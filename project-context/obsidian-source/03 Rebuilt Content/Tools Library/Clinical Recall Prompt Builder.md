# Clinical Recall Prompt Builder

Status: Draft v1  
Page type: Tool page

---

## Purpose

The Clinical Recall Prompt Builder helps you turn facts, notes, scenario errors, directive details, and confusing concepts into recall prompts that support clinical use.

Use it when you do not just want to remember an answer.

Use it when you want the knowledge to help you notice, decide, withhold, reassess, or explain something during a call, lab, scenario, or OSCE.

This tool is not an Anki tool by itself.

You can use the prompts in Anki if you want, but the purpose is broader than flashcards. The purpose is to shape recall so it stays connected to patient care.

---

## When to Use

Use this tool when:

- a fact keeps showing up but does not feel clinically useful yet
- a Smart Note needs to become easier to retrieve
- a scenario exposed something you could not remember under pressure
- a directive feels like wording instead of a decision structure
- you are making Anki cards and want to avoid shallow prompts
- you can remember a definition but struggle to use the idea
- you want to study a condition, medication, or assessment finding in a more clinical way

You do not need to use this for every detail.

Use it for material that should become easier to reach during patient assessment, decision-making, reassessment, or communication.

---

## The Builder

Choose one fact, concept, scenario error, directive detail, or Smart Note.

Then move through the questions below.

---

### 1. What am I trying to remember?

Name the fact or concept.

Keep it small.

Examples:

- nitroglycerin safety
- quiet lung sounds in severe asthma
- early signs of poor perfusion
- reassessment after bronchodilator treatment
- altered mental status in shortness of breath
- why a contraindication matters
- chest pain with a non-diagnostic first ECG

If the topic feels too large, narrow it before making prompts.

“Respiratory distress” is probably too big.

“Signs that a respiratory patient is tiring” is more useful.

---

### 2. What clinical job does this knowledge do?

Ask what this knowledge helps you do.

Choose one or more:

- notice something
- decide something
- avoid harm
- reassess something
- explain something
- compare similar presentations
- recognize a boundary
- change the plan

This step matters because it keeps recall connected to use.

A fact without a clinical job may still be important, but it may belong in reference material or a simple fact card rather than a clinical recall prompt.

---

### 3. Make one basic fact prompt

Start with the clean detail.

This is the simple recall version.

Examples:

- What is the adult dose of ASA?
- What is a contraindication to nitroglycerin?
- What does bronchoconstriction mean?
- What is perfusion?
- What vital signs should be reassessed after treatment?

Basic fact prompts are not bad.

They just should not be the whole system.

---

### 4. Make one clinical cue prompt

Now connect the idea to what you might see, hear, ask, or notice.

Examples:

- What findings might suggest poor perfusion before blood pressure drops?
- What makes quieter lung sounds concerning in severe asthma?
- What patient comments might make chest pain sound higher risk?
- What would make a shortness of breath call feel less stable?
- What findings suggest a patient is tiring despite treatment?

Clinical cue prompts help knowledge show up during assessment.

They train you to notice, not just define.

---

### 5. Make one decision or boundary prompt

Now connect the idea to action, withholding, caution, or scope.

Examples:

- What must be assessed before giving nitroglycerin?
- What would make this medication unsafe or inappropriate?
- What would make me patch, stop, or change course?
- What finding would make this patient higher priority?
- What is outside my scope here?
- What would make this directive no longer fit?

Decision and boundary prompts help prevent overconfident action and unnecessary hesitation.

They connect recall to judgment.

---

### 6. Make one reassessment prompt

Now ask what should be checked again after time, treatment, or new information.

Examples:

- What should be reassessed after giving a bronchodilator?
- What should change if oxygen is helping?
- What would make me question my first impression?
- What would I reassess before repeating nitroglycerin?
- What findings would suggest the patient is worsening despite treatment?
- What would make my current explanation weaker?

Reassessment prompts are especially useful in paramedicine because calls change.

A recall system that does not train reassessment can make knowledge feel static.

Patient care is not static.

---

### 7. Optional: Make one communication prompt

Use this when the idea needs to be explained during a scenario, OSCE, patch, handover, or partner discussion.

Examples:

- How would I explain why this chest pain still concerns me?
- How would I explain why I am withholding this medication?
- How would I explain what I am watching for after treatment?
- How would I explain why this respiratory patient may be tiring?
- How would I summarize my concern without needing a perfect diagnosis?

Communication prompts help turn internal reasoning into usable language.

This matters because students often know more than they can say clearly under pressure.

## Short Version

Use this structure:

- What am I trying to remember?
- What clinical job does it do?
- Basic fact prompt:
- Clinical cue prompt:
- Decision or boundary prompt:
- Reassessment prompt:
- Optional communication prompt:

That is enough.

Do not make this heavier than it needs to be.

## Example: Nitroglycerin

### What am I trying to remember?

Nitroglycerin safety and reassessment.

### What clinical job does it do?

It helps me decide whether nitroglycerin is appropriate, what would make it unsafe, and what needs reassessment before repeating it.

### Basic fact prompt

What is the dose of nitroglycerin?

### Clinical cue prompt

What patient presentation might make nitroglycerin relevant?

### Decision or boundary prompt

What must I assess before giving nitroglycerin, and what would make me pause or withhold it?

### Reassessment prompt

What should I reassess before considering another dose of nitroglycerin?

### Communication prompt

How would I explain why I am not giving nitroglycerin right now?

---

## Example: Respiratory Fatigue

### What am I trying to remember?

Signs that a respiratory patient is tiring.

### What clinical job does it do?

It helps me notice worsening respiratory status even when one finding, such as less wheezing, might look falsely reassuring.

### Basic fact prompt

What does increased work of breathing mean?

### Clinical cue prompt

What findings suggest a respiratory patient is becoming tired?

### Decision or boundary prompt

What would make ventilation support more urgent?

### Reassessment prompt

What should I reassess after oxygen, positioning, or bronchodilator treatment?

### Communication prompt

How would I explain that this patient is worsening even if the wheezing sounds quieter?

---

## Example: Poor Perfusion

### What am I trying to remember?

Poor perfusion can appear before hypotension.

### What clinical job does it do?

It helps me notice early instability instead of waiting for a dramatic blood pressure change.

### Basic fact prompt

What is perfusion?

### Clinical cue prompt

What early findings might suggest poor perfusion before blood pressure falls?

### Decision or boundary prompt

What findings would make this patient higher priority?

### Reassessment prompt

What should I reassess if I am concerned about perfusion?

### Communication prompt

How would I explain that the patient may be compensating even though the blood pressure is still acceptable?

## How to Use This With Smart Notes

Smart Notes are a good source for clinical recall prompts.

A Smart Note usually already contains:

- a claim
- an explanation
- clinical signals
- common confusion
- links

Use those pieces to build prompts.

The claim may become your basic fact prompt.

The clinical signals may become cue prompts.

The common confusion may become a decision or boundary prompt.

The links may help you make comparison prompts.

Do not copy the whole Smart Note into a flashcard.

Pull out the parts that need stronger access.

---

## How to Use This With Anki

This tool can help you make better Anki cards, but it should not become an Anki setup system.

Use it before making cards.

Ask what kind of recall the topic needs, then create a small number of cards from the prompts.

For an important topic, you might make:

- one basic fact card
- one clinical cue card
- one decision or boundary card
- one reassessment card

That is often more useful than making ten disconnected definition cards.

Keep the cards short enough to answer.

If a prompt becomes too long, split it into smaller pieces.

---

## Common Mistakes

### Making every prompt too large

Clinical recall does not mean every card or prompt needs to be complex.

If the prompt asks for too much, it becomes hard to answer and hard to review.

Keep prompts small.

Use several connected prompts instead of one overloaded prompt.

---

### Skipping basic facts entirely

Clinical recall still needs accurate facts.

Do not avoid basic recall because it feels too simple.

Medication doses, contraindications, assessment steps, and key values may still need clean access.

The goal is balance.

Simple facts plus clinical use.

---

### Making prompts that only work because the wording is familiar

If you always ask the same question in the same wording, you may start recognizing the prompt instead of retrieving the idea.

For important concepts, vary the angle.

Ask about the cue once, the decision once, and reassessment once.

---

### Turning every scenario into cards

Do not convert the entire scenario into a deck.

Choose one or two moments where better access would have helped.

That is usually enough.

---

### Forgetting reassessment

Many students make prompts about what to do first.

Fewer make prompts about what to check afterward.

In paramedicine, reassessment is where your thinking stays honest.

Make reassessment prompts on purpose.
