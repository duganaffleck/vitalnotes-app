# Directive Meaning Check

Status: Draft v1  
Page type: Tool page

---

## Purpose

The Directive Meaning Check helps you learn a directive by understanding what it is protecting, supporting, or preventing.

Use it when a directive feels like wording you are trying to survive instead of a decision structure you understand.

This tool does not replace the directive.

You still need to know the indications, contraindications, dose, route, conditions, patch points, reassessment expectations, and documentation requirements. The purpose of this tool is to help those details make clinical sense.

---

## When to Use

Use this tool when:

- a directive feels hard to remember
- a directive feels intimidating during scenarios or OSCEs
- you can recite the wording but struggle to explain the decision
- you are unsure why a contraindication or threshold matters
- you keep hesitating when the patient does not fit the clean version you studied
- you want to turn a directive into a more useful study note

You do not need to use it for every directive every time.

It is most useful when the directive feels fragile, confusing, or hard to apply.

---

## The Check

Choose one directive or one part of a directive.

Then answer these questions.

---

### 1. What clinical risk is this directive protecting against?

Name the patient risk underneath the directive.

Do not start with the wording. Start with the danger.

Examples of risk:

- worsening oxygenation
- poor perfusion
- untreated pain
- worsening bronchospasm
- medication harm
- missed deterioration
- unsafe refusal
- delayed transport

---

### 2. What physiology is being supported or protected?

Ask what body process the directive is trying to help, stabilize, or avoid worsening.

Examples:

- supporting oxygenation
- improving ventilation
- reducing cardiac workload
- supporting perfusion
- preventing further deterioration
- treating symptoms that interfere with safe care
- avoiding harm when the patient is not a safe candidate

---

### 3. Where are the firm boundaries?

List the parts that are not flexible.

Include:

- indications
- contraindications
- dose
- route
- age limits
- vital sign limits
- required assessments
- patch points
- maximums
- reassessment requirements
- documentation requirements

The goal here is not to make the directive loose.

The goal is to understand why the boundaries matter.

---

### 4. What would make me withhold, stop, patch, or change course?

Name the things that would make the treatment unsafe, inappropriate, incomplete, or no longer fitting the patient.

Ask:

- What finding would make this unsafe?
- What information do I still need before acting?
- What change after treatment would concern me?
- What would make me reassess before continuing?
- What would require consultation, patching, or transport priority change?

---

### 5. What should I reassess afterward?

A directive is not finished once the intervention is performed.

Ask what should change if the intervention helped.

Reassess things like:

- symptoms
- vital signs
- mental status
- work of breathing
- pain severity
- nausea or vomiting
- skin signs
- ECG findings where relevant
- adverse effects
- patient ability to continue with care or transport

---

## Short Version

When studying a directive, ask:

- What risk is this directive built around?
- What physiology or patient problem is being supported?
- What are the firm boundaries?
- What would make this unsafe or inappropriate?
- What needs to be reassessed afterward?

---

## Example: Oxygen Administration

A student might memorize oxygen as a number-based decision.

That is not enough.

A Directive Meaning Check asks what oxygen is actually meant to support.

### Clinical risk

The patient may not be oxygenating adequately, or may be at risk of worsening oxygenation based on their presentation.

### Physiology

Oxygen supports oxygen delivery when oxygenation is inadequate or threatened.

### Firm boundaries

The student must consider the directive, patient presentation, oxygen saturation, reliability of the reading, work of breathing, mental status, skin signs, and response to care.

### Withhold, change, or reassess

Oxygen should not be applied automatically just because a patient says they are short of breath. The student should ask whether oxygen is indicated, whether the reading is reliable, and whether the patient’s overall presentation suggests risk.

### Reassessment

After oxygen is applied or adjusted, reassess:

- oxygen saturation
- work of breathing
- respiratory rate
- mental status
- skin signs
- patient comfort
- whether the patient is improving, worsening, or unchanged

The point is not to chase a number.

The point is to understand whether oxygen is supporting the patient problem in front of you.

---

## How to Use This in Your Notes

After reviewing a directive, write a short note using this structure:

- This directive is mainly protecting against:
- It supports or affects:
- The firm boundaries are:
- I would withhold, stop, patch, or change course if:
- After treatment, I need to reassess:

Keep it short.

A few clear sentences are better than rewriting the whole directive.
