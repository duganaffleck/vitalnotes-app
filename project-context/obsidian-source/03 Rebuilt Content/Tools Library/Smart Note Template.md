# Smart Note Template

Status: Draft v1  
Page type: Tool page

---

## Purpose

The Smart Note Template helps you turn a concept, scenario error, confusing idea, or repeated feedback point into a small reusable note.

Use it when something feels important enough to keep developing, not when you are trying to capture everything.

A Smart Note is not a lecture summary.

It is a thinking support.

Its job is to help future you understand, connect, retrieve, and use one idea.

---

## When to Use

Use this tool when:

- a scenario exposed a mistake you want to understand
- a concept keeps showing up in different contexts
- a directive decision feels fragile
- a physiological mechanism finally starts to make sense
- you keep confusing two similar presentations
- feedback repeats across labs or scenarios
- a small clinical distinction would change assessment, treatment, or reassessment

You do not need to use this template for every note.

Use it when an idea is worth keeping.

---

## The Template

### 1. Claim

Write the core idea in one clear sentence.

This should be small enough to reuse later.

Examples:

- Quiet lungs can mean worsening respiratory fatigue.
- Early shock may appear before hypotension.
- Reassessment after treatment tells you whether your explanation still fits.
- Nausea can be a symptom, a treatment issue, or a clue to a larger problem.
- A contraindication usually points to a risk, not just a rule.

The claim should not be the title of a lecture.

It should be the idea you want future you to remember.

---

### 2. Explanation

Explain the idea in your own words.

Keep it short.

Ask:

- What is happening?
- Why does it matter?
- What mechanism or decision does it connect to?
- Why would a student miss this?
- How would this change what I do next time?

Do not copy the textbook definition unless you need it for accuracy.

The goal is to make the idea usable.

---

### 3. Clinical Signals

List what this idea might look like in assessment, scenarios, or patient care.

Ask:

- What would I see?
- What would I hear?
- What would the patient say?
- What would change over time?
- What vital signs, history, physical findings, or behaviours matter?
- What reassessment would help confirm or challenge this idea?

Keep this practical.

Clinical signals help the note connect back to the patient.

---

### 4. Common Confusion

Name the mistake, mix-up, or trap this note is meant to prevent.

Ask:

- What do students often confuse here?
- What looks similar but means something different?
- What might make me relax too early?
- What might make me overreact?
- What detail changes the decision?
- What did I miss in the scenario or debrief?

This section is important.

A Smart Note becomes more useful when it protects you from a real mistake.

---

### 5. Links

Connect this note to a few related ideas.

Only link notes that change how you understand or use this idea.

Good links might connect to:

- a mechanism
- a similar presentation
- a directive
- a scenario error
- a reassessment habit
- a clinical reasoning pattern
- a common confusion

Do not link just because two notes are in the same topic.

A link should help future you follow a reasoning trail.

---

## Short Version

Use this structure:

- Claim:
- Explanation:
- Clinical signals:
- Common confusion:
- Links:

That is enough.

A Smart Note does not need to be long.

## Example: Respiratory Fatigue

### Claim

Quiet lungs can mean worsening respiratory fatigue.

### Explanation

In severe bronchospasm or respiratory distress, less wheezing is not always improvement. If the patient is working hard to breathe and then becomes quieter, more tired, less able to speak, or more altered, the issue may be reduced air movement rather than recovery.

### Clinical Signals

- decreasing ability to speak
- persistent or worsening work of breathing
- reduced air movement
- fatigue after initial treatment
- altered mental status
- poor response to bronchodilator treatment
- declining respiratory effectiveness despite less obvious wheeze

### Common Confusion

Students may hear less wheezing and assume the patient is improving.

The safer question is whether air movement, effort, speech, mental status, and overall trajectory are improving together.

## Example: Directive Decision

### Claim

A contraindication usually points to a risk, not just a rule.

### Explanation

Contraindications are not random barriers. They usually exist because a treatment could harm the patient or make the situation worse under certain conditions. Understanding the risk behind the contraindication makes the directive easier to remember and safer to apply.

### Clinical Signals

- borderline vital signs
- incomplete history
- medication use that changes eligibility
- patient condition changing after treatment
- uncertainty about whether the directive still fits
- need for reassessment, withholding, patching, or changing course

### Common Confusion

Students may treat contraindications as checklist items to recite rather than risks to understand.

This can lead to either unsafe treatment or unnecessary hesitation.

## How to Use This After a Scenario

After a scenario or lab, do not try to write notes on everything.

Choose one moment.

Good prompts:

- What did I miss that mattered?
- What did I know but fail to use?
- What feedback keeps repeating?
- What distinction would have changed my assessment?
- What should I recognize earlier next time?

Then turn that one idea into a Smart Note.

Do not write a full reflection unless you need to.

One useful note is enough.

---

## How to Use This While Studying

When studying, look for ideas that explain other ideas.

Good candidates:

- mechanisms that show up across conditions
- findings that change meaning depending on context
- common confusions between similar presentations
- directive decisions that depend on risk
- reassessment points after treatment
- patient cues that suggest deterioration or compensation

Do not turn every paragraph into a Smart Note.

Most information can stay in rough notes.

Smart Notes are for ideas worth reusing.

---

## What Makes a Good Smart Note

A good Smart Note is:

- small
- clear
- written in your own words
- connected to patient care
- useful beyond one test
- linked to related ideas
- easy to revise later

A good Smart Note helps future you think faster, not because it gives you a shortcut, but because some of the meaning has already been built.

---

## What to Avoid

Avoid:

- copying lecture slides
- making one note too large
- trying to capture everything
- building complex folder systems before you have useful notes
- writing notes only to prove effort
- adding links that do not change understanding
- making the template so heavy that you stop using it

The template should reduce friction.

If it becomes another assignment, simplify it.
