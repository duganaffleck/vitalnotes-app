## App-Ready Section Draft

## Why feedback often does not change the next scenario

Most students do not ignore feedback.

They hear it. They nod. They understand what the instructor is saying. Sometimes they agree with the feedback completely. Then the next scenario starts, the room gets busy again, and the same issue comes back.

That can feel discouraging.

It can also feel confusing, because the feedback seemed clear at the time. The student knew what went wrong. They may have been able to explain it afterward. They may have left the room genuinely intending to fix it.

The missing step is usually not caring.

The missing step is conversion.

Feedback has to be converted into something small enough to practise while the next call is unfolding. Otherwise, it stays as a general correction floating around in the student’s head.

After one scenario, a student might be told to:

- reassess sooner
- explain risk more clearly
- make a transport decision earlier
- check contraindications more cleanly
- stop closing too quickly on the first diagnosis
- communicate more effectively with the patient or partner

All of that feedback may be accurate.

It is also too much to carry all at once.

The question after feedback is not, “How do I fix everything?”

A more useful question is, “What is the next adjustment I can actually test?”

## Feedback is not practice yet

Feedback identifies a gap. Practice changes how the student responds to that gap next time.

Those are connected, but they are not the same thing.

An instructor might say, “You lost reassessment after the first intervention.” That is useful feedback. It names something important. But the student still needs to decide what the feedback means in practical terms.

Do they need to rehearse a reassessment loop?  
Do they need to say their reassessment plan out loud?  
Do they need to connect each treatment to the finding that justified it?  
Do they need to stop thinking of treatment as the end of the decision?

Until feedback becomes a specific adjustment, it is too broad to guide the next attempt.

This is one reason students can understand feedback and still repeat the same error. They are not necessarily resisting the correction. They may simply not have turned it into a practice target.

## One adjustment is usually enough

After a difficult scenario, students often want to fix everything immediately.

That impulse makes sense. Nobody likes leaving a room feeling exposed, slow, or unsafe in their thinking. A student may want to prove that they took the feedback seriously by writing down every issue and promising to work on all of it.

The problem is that scenario performance already carries a high cognitive load.

Adding six new goals into the next scenario usually makes performance more fragile. Attention splits. The student becomes self-conscious. They monitor themselves instead of the patient. They may become so focused on avoiding the last mistake that they stop seeing the call they are currently in.

Focused practice works differently.

It chooses one adjustment and gives it enough room to show up.

One adjustment might be:

- naming a working concern earlier
- reassessing after each intervention
- checking what does not fit the first pattern
- explaining risk in plain language
- making a transport decision once risk is recognized
- linking a directive decision to patient findings rather than memory alone

This does not mean the rest of the scenario stops mattering. The student still has to manage the patient safely.

It means one part of the performance is being deliberately tested.

That is usually enough for one attempt.

## A paramedic example

Consider a student who has just finished a respiratory scenario.

The treatment was reasonable. They recognized distress, initiated care, communicated with their partner, and started preparing for transport. Nothing about the call was careless.

But there was a repeated issue.

After treatment, the student kept moving forward without clearly checking whether the patient had actually improved. They did not return to the work of breathing, speaking ability, lung sounds, mental status, or overall appearance that made them intervene in the first place.

The instructor says, “Your treatment made sense, but you did not close the loop. You need to reassess after you intervene.”

The student understands the comment.

They might write down:

“Reassess more.”

That is not wrong, but it is not quite usable yet.

A better practice target would be:

“After any respiratory intervention, I will reassess the finding that made me intervene: work of breathing, speaking ability, lung sounds, SpO₂ trend if reliable, mental status, and patient appearance.”

Now the feedback has changed shape.

It is no longer a general reminder. It is a specific behaviour tied to a clinical reason.

In the next scenario, the student does not need to become perfect at every part of respiratory care. They need to test whether they can close the loop after treatment while still managing the rest of the call.

That is focused practice.

## What makes a practice target useful

A good practice target is specific enough to test.

It should answer three questions:

1. What will I notice?
2. What will I do differently?
3. Where will I test it next?

A weak target sounds like this:

“I need to improve communication.”

That may be true, but it is too broad to practise well.

A stronger target sounds like this:

“In the next scenario, I will explain my working concern to my partner before moving to treatment or transport.”

A weak target:

“I need better clinical reasoning.”

A stronger target:

“When I form an early impression, I will name one finding that supports it and one finding that could challenge it.”

A weak target:

“I need to be less nervous with directives.”

A stronger target:

“When reviewing a directive, I will identify the clinical risk it is protecting against, then practise applying it to one borderline patient example.”

The goal is not to make the target sound impressive. The goal is to make it clear enough that the student can actually use it when the next scenario starts.

## Practice targets have to survive pressure

A practice target that only works when the student is calm and unrushed is probably too large.

Good targets are small enough to survive a real lab environment. They have to be usable while the student is managing a patient, hearing new information, talking to a partner, and watching the scene change.

This is why wording matters.

“Improve prioritization” is too large.

“Name the primary risk before collecting more history” is smaller.

“Use better reassessment” is too broad.

“After treatment, recheck the finding that justified the treatment” is smaller.

“Stop premature closure” is too abstract.

“Ask what does not fit before committing to the first explanation” is smaller.

Small does not mean shallow. In paramedicine, small adjustments often change the direction of the whole call because they redirect attention at the moment where thinking usually starts to drift.

## How to extract one adjustment from feedback

After a scenario, feedback can come quickly. Sometimes it is organized. Sometimes several people comment at once. Sometimes the feedback is accurate but hard to absorb because the scenario already felt rough.

The student does not need to capture every word.

The job is to extract the adjustment.

Use this sequence:

1. Listen for the repeated pattern.
2. Identify the moment where the pattern showed up.
3. Translate the feedback into one behaviour.
4. Decide where that behaviour will be tested next.

For example:

Feedback:

“You kept gathering information, but you never really changed your plan.”

Pattern:

Waiting for certainty.

Moment:

The patient was trending worse, but assessment continued as if the call were still low risk.

Practice target:

“When a patient trends worse, I will name the working concern and decide whether my transport priority needs to change.”

Next test:

The next scenario involving vague symptoms, abnormal vitals, or a patient who changes over time.

That is enough.

The student does not need a full essay after every run. They need one usable adjustment.

## Focused practice is not just doing more scenarios

More practice can help, but only if something about the practice changes.

A student can repeat the same error many times. They can even become smoother at repeating it. This is why “just do more scenarios” is incomplete advice.

Focused practice means repeating with attention to one specific change.

If the issue is premature closure, the student should not simply run more chest pain or respiratory scenarios. They should practise holding an early impression while still checking for what does not fit.

If the issue is missed reassessment, the student should not only review treatment steps. They should practise linking every intervention to a follow-up assessment.

If the issue is directive hesitation, the student should not only reread the directive. They should practise applying the directive to realistic patient presentations, including borderline or changing cases.

The point is not more practice for its own sake.

The point is more precise practice.

## Why focused practice feels awkward at first

Focused practice can make a student feel less smooth for a while.

That is normal.

When a student starts paying deliberate attention to one part of performance, the call may feel slower. They may pause more. They may speak their reasoning more carefully. They may feel like they are moving backward because something that used to run on habit is now being examined on purpose.

This does not mean the practice target is wrong.

It means the student has made the weak point visible.

That visibility is uncomfortable, especially when the target involves clinical reasoning, reassessment, communication, or transport decisions. These are not isolated skills. They are woven into the whole call.

At first, the student is learning to notice the moment.

Later, they learn to act in the moment.

With enough useful repetition, the adjustment starts to feel less like an added task and more like part of how they practise.

## A second example: premature closure

A student completes a scenario involving shortness of breath.

The patient is wheezy, anxious, and sitting upright. The student quickly identifies asthma and begins treatment. Some of this is reasonable. The presentation does resemble asthma.

The issue is not that the student noticed a pattern.

The issue is that they stopped testing it.

They did not pay enough attention to chest discomfort, poor response to treatment, skin signs, or the possibility that this was not a straightforward asthma exacerbation. They kept trying to make the call fit the first explanation.

Feedback identifies premature closure.

A weak practice response would be:

“I need to stop assuming.”

That is understandable, but it is not very useful.

A stronger practice target would be:

“When I recognize a familiar pattern early, I will name one finding that supports it and one finding that would make me reconsider.”

That target does not tell the student to ignore pattern recognition. It teaches them to keep it accountable.

In the next respiratory scenario, the student can test that adjustment directly. They can still act on the likely problem, but they must keep checking whether the patient is behaving as expected.

That is how focused practice protects clinical reasoning.

## Feedback should change what the student notices

A good practice target often changes attention before it changes action.

This matters.

Students sometimes assume improvement means doing something new. Sometimes it does. But often, improvement begins by noticing the right thing sooner.

A student working on reassessment starts noticing what changes after intervention.

A student working on transport decisions starts noticing trends earlier.

A student working on communication starts noticing when the patient does not understand the risk.

A student working on directive application starts noticing whether the clinical picture actually matches the reason the directive exists.

Attention comes first.

Action follows.

This is why focused practice should not be treated like a checklist. The student is training what to notice, when to notice it, and how to respond once it appears.

## When feedback gives you too much

Sometimes feedback is accurate but too large.

An instructor may identify several issues at once:

- assessment was scattered
- history was incomplete
- reassessment was weak
- communication with the partner was unclear
- transport decision was delayed

The student may leave feeling like the whole scenario failed.

In that moment, the useful move is to look for the issue underneath several of the comments.

If assessment was scattered, reassessment was weak, and transport was delayed, the deeper issue may be loss of prioritization. The student was doing tasks, but not organizing them around the main clinical risk.

The practice target might become:

“After my first set of findings, I will name the main risk and use that to guide what I ask, reassess, or do next.”

That one target may improve several visible behaviours because it addresses the structure underneath them.

This is not ignoring feedback. It is organizing it so it can be practised.

## When to practise outside the scenario

Not every practice target has to begin inside a full scenario.

Some targets can be strengthened in smaller pieces first.

If the target is directive decision-making, the student can practise with short patient examples.

If the target is risk explanation, the student can rehearse explaining risk in plain language.

If the target is reassessment, the student can build quick intervention-reassessment pairs.

If the target is clinical reasoning, the student can compare two similar cases and ask what finding would change the plan.

Smaller practice helps reduce load. It lets the student strengthen one part of performance before adding the full pressure of a scenario.

Then the adjustment still needs to be tested in context.

That second part matters.

Practice outside the scenario prepares the adjustment. Scenario practice tests whether it holds.

## How to know if practice is working

Focused practice is working when the pattern starts to change.

That change may be small at first.

The scenario may still feel uneven. The student may still make mistakes. But something should be different.

Possible signs include:

- the student notices the issue sooner
- the student catches the mistake while it is happening
- the student asks a better question at the right moment
- the student adjusts after feedback instead of repeating the same pattern unchanged
- the student can explain what they were trying to improve
- the student recovers faster after losing track

Early improvement often looks like recovery, not perfection.

That matters because students sometimes dismiss progress if the whole scenario still felt messy. But if the repeated error changed shape, learning is happening.

The goal is not to leave every scenario feeling good.

The goal is to leave with evidence that practice is affecting performance.

## What to avoid after feedback

There are a few common traps after feedback.

### Trying to fix everything

This creates overload and usually leads to shallow change.

Choose one adjustment.

### Turning feedback into self-criticism

Self-criticism can feel active, but it rarely improves the next attempt on its own.

Translate the feedback into behaviour.

### Practising only what feels comfortable

Students often repeat what they already do well because it restores confidence.

Spend some time where the pattern is actually weak.

### Treating the next scenario as a chance to prove yourself

The next scenario is not only a performance. It is also a test of the adjustment.

The question is not, “Can I be flawless now?”

A better question is, “Can I apply the one thing I said I would practise?”

## A simple feedback-to-practice sequence

Use this after a scenario or lab when feedback feels important but too broad.

1. Name the pattern.

What kind of error showed up?

2. Choose one adjustment.

What is the smallest useful change?

3. Decide where it will show up.

In what kind of scenario, patient, or moment will this matter?

4. Test it deliberately.

Carry that adjustment into the next attempt.

5. Check whether it changed anything.

Did you notice it sooner, act differently, or recover faster?

This sequence is small on purpose. It is meant to survive real lab days, not become another assignment.

## Moving forward

Feedback is only useful if it changes practice.

That does not mean every correction needs a full reflection, a new system, or a long plan. Most of the time, the next step is smaller than that.

Read the feedback. Find the pattern. Choose one adjustment. Test it.

The adjustment may not fix everything right away. It may feel awkward at first. It may need several attempts before it becomes stable.

That is still productive work.

## Glossary and Popup Terms

### focused practice

Practice aimed at improving one specific target, rather than trying to improve everything at once.

### practice target

A specific behaviour, decision, or attention habit that a student deliberately carries into the next attempt.

### feedback extraction

The process of turning feedback into one clear next adjustment.

### deliberate practice

Focused repetition aimed at improving a specific weakness, with feedback and repeated testing.

### transfer

The ability to use something learned in one setting, such as feedback or study, in another setting, such as a scenario or clinical decision.

### calibration

The process of adjusting confidence and judgment based on evidence from performance and feedback.

### reassessment loop

The habit of linking an intervention to a follow-up check that asks whether the patient improved, worsened, or stayed the same.
