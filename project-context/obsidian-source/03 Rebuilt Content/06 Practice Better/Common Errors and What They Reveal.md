## App-Ready Section Draft

## Why common errors feel personal

After a rough scenario, it is easy for a student to turn one mistake into a much bigger story.

I missed the reassessment.  
I froze on the directive.  
I got pulled into the wrong diagnosis.  
I knew better and still did it wrong.

That last part is usually the hardest. Many scenario mistakes happen in areas students have already studied. They may know the concept when sitting at a desk. They may be able to explain it clearly afterward. They may even recognize the mistake as soon as the scenario ends.

That does not make the error meaningless. It means the problem may not be knowledge alone.

In paramedicine, performance depends on what a student can notice, retrieve, prioritize, and adjust while the call is still moving. Scenario days expose that system. They show where understanding is usable and where it is still fragile.

A common error is not something to excuse. It is something to read carefully.

## Occasional mistakes and repeated patterns are different

Not every mistake reveals a deep issue.

Sometimes a student mishears a number, phrases a question awkwardly, forgets a small step once, or gets disrupted by something happening in the room. Those moments still matter, but they do not always tell the whole story.

Repeated errors are different.

If the same kind of mistake appears across scenarios, the details may change while the shape stays familiar.

A student might:

- delay transport while waiting for a clearer diagnosis
- stop reassessing after the first intervention
- focus on a skill and lose the larger patient picture
- treat a directive like a fragile memory test instead of a decision support tool
- lock onto the first familiar pattern and ignore details that do not fit
- gather more and more history without naming the main concern

Those patterns are worth paying attention to. They usually show where the learning system needs more support.

## What errors can reveal

A useful error review asks a better question than “What did I do wrong?”

It asks, “What does this error reveal?”

Different errors point to different problems.

A missed medication check may reveal that a procedural habit is not yet stable under pressure.

A delayed transport decision may reveal that the student is waiting for certainty before acting on risk.

A weak reassessment may reveal that the student sees treatment as the endpoint, rather than the start of the next assessment cycle.

A premature diagnosis may reveal that pattern recognition is moving faster than verification.

A scattered history may reveal cognitive overload, not laziness or lack of caring.

This distinction matters because each problem needs a different response. Studying harder does not fix every error. Sometimes the student needs retrieval practice. Sometimes they need a clearer mental model. Sometimes they need to rehearse one decision point until it becomes easier to access under pressure. Sometimes they need to simplify how they enter a scenario because their attention is being used up too early.

The error helps show where to look.

## A paramedic example

Consider a student working through a scenario involving an older patient with abdominal pain, nausea, and vague weakness.

The student is careful. They complete a primary assessment, ask a detailed history, check medications, and repeat parts of the abdominal exam. Their approach is not careless. They are trying to be thorough and safe.

But the patient looks worse over time.

The blood pressure trends downward. Skin becomes cooler. The patient is increasingly uncomfortable and less able to answer clearly. Nothing has become perfectly obvious, but the overall picture has changed.

The student continues gathering information, hoping the scenario will eventually point to a clean answer.

The visible error might be described as delayed transport priority.

The deeper pattern is more specific:

The student is treating uncertainty as a reason to keep assessing, when uncertainty should be changing the plan.

In a case like this, the student does not need a perfect diagnosis before acting. They need to name the risk, adjust urgency, reassess deliberately, and move toward a safer plan. The concern might be abdominal sepsis, internal bleeding, an aneurysm, bowel obstruction, or something else entirely. The point is not to be certain early. The point is to recognize that the patient is no longer behaving like a low-risk assessment.

Once that pattern is named, the next practice target becomes clearer.

Not:

“Be better at abdominal pain.”

More useful:

“In the next scenario, if an older patient looks unwell and trends worse, I will name the working concern earlier and decide what action keeps them safest while I clarify.”

That is something a student can actually carry into the next room.

## Why good students repeat errors

Repeated errors can be frustrating because they often come from reasonable instincts.

A careful student may delay action because they do not want to overreact.

A thorough student may gather too much information because they want to be accurate.

A cautious student may hesitate with directives because they understand that protocol errors matter.

A confident student may commit early because they recognize a familiar pattern and want to move efficiently.

Those instincts are not bad on their own. In fact, they are often part of what makes the student conscientious. The problem is that each instinct can be pushed too far under pressure.

Caution can become delay.  
Thoroughness can become overload.  
Confidence can become premature closure.  
Protocol respect can become paralysis.

Common errors often reveal where balance is not yet stable.

That is why feedback can feel uncomfortable. The instructor may not be asking the student to care more. They may be asking the student to use their care differently.

## The difference between correction and learning

Correction tells you what should have happened.

Learning changes what happens next time.

Those are related, but they are not the same.

After a scenario, feedback might sound like this:

- “You needed to reassess after the treatment.”
- “You waited too long to make a transport decision.”
- “You closed too early on asthma.”
- “You did not explain the risk clearly enough to the patient.”
- “You knew the directive, but you did not apply it cleanly.”

That feedback identifies the issue. It does not automatically create the fix.

To make feedback useful, the student has to translate the correction into a practice target.

For example:

- “After any intervention, I will deliberately reassess the finding that made me intervene.”
- “When I notice a worsening trend, I will name transport priority before collecting more detail.”
- “When a presentation looks familiar, I will identify one feature that does not fit.”
- “When a patient hesitates or refuses, I will explain risk in plain language before asking for agreement.”
- “For directives, I will practise the decision point, not just the wording.”

This is where feedback starts to become usable. It changes the next attempt.

## Avoiding the error catalogue trap

It can be tempting to make a long list of mistakes and try to fix all of them.

That usually fails.

A long error list creates noise. It makes students feel busy without making practice sharper. It can also create defensive learning, where the student becomes focused on not doing anything wrong instead of thinking clearly and acting safely.

The goal is not to collect every error.

The goal is to identify the pattern that matters most right now.

A useful question after feedback is:

“What is the one error pattern most likely to affect my next scenario if I do not address it?”

That question narrows attention. It also protects learning from becoming another source of overload.

## How to read an error pattern

When an error repeats, pause long enough to look underneath it.

Ask:

1. What was the visible mistake?
2. Has this happened before in a similar form?
3. What was happening to my attention at the time?
4. What assumption was guiding me?
5. What would I need to practise so this changes next time?

The answer should lead to a practice target, not a personality judgment.

If the answer is “I need to be better,” it is too vague.

If the answer is “I need to reassess after treatment,” it is closer.

If the answer is “After giving a treatment, I will reassess the specific finding that made me give it, then decide whether the patient is improving, unchanged, or worse,” it is useful.

That final version gives the next scenario something to test.

## Common patterns worth noticing

The point of this list is not to memorize more mistakes. It is to recognize the shape of a problem when it appears.

### Waiting for certainty

This pattern shows up when students keep assessing because they want the decision to become obvious.

It often appears in vague abdominal pain, weakness, dizziness, shortness of breath, altered mental status, or early shock. The student may have enough information to act safely, but they keep searching for confirmation.

The practice target is not simply speed.

It is risk naming.

The student needs to practise saying, “I do not know exactly what this is yet, but the risk is high enough that my plan needs to change.”

### Losing reassessment

This pattern shows up after an intervention.

The student gives oxygen, ventilates, administers a medication, moves the patient, changes position, or completes a skill, then continues forward without checking whether the original problem improved.

This can happen because treatment feels like completion.

In paramedicine, treatment should create the next question.

Did that help?  
Did it fail?  
Did it create a new concern?  
Did the patient change in a way that alters the plan?

The practice target is a simple reassessment loop:

Intervene.  
Recheck the reason you intervened.  
Adjust.

### Fixating on the first familiar explanation

This pattern appears when a patient resembles something the student has seen before.

Wheezing becomes asthma. Chest pain becomes ACS. Anxiety becomes panic. Weakness becomes “general unwell.” Intoxication becomes the whole explanation.

Sometimes the first pattern is correct. The risk is closing the case before checking what does not fit.

The practice target is verification.

The student should practise asking, “What finding would make this pattern unsafe to trust?”

### Treating directives as memory tests

This pattern appears when students know a directive, but freeze when the patient does not fit perfectly.

They search for exact wording instead of thinking about the clinical risk the directive is managing.

This does not mean wording is unimportant. It is important. But wording alone does not create judgment.

The practice target is directive meaning.

The student should practise asking:

- What risk is this directive protecting against?
- What findings matter most?
- What would make this unsafe?
- What reassessment is required after action or withholding?

This turns the directive from a fragile script into a safer decision frame.

### Letting skills consume the call

This pattern appears when a procedure takes over attention.

The student becomes focused on getting a blood pressure, setting up equipment, preparing a medication, placing leads, moving the patient, or performing a skill cleanly. Meanwhile, the broader clinical picture drifts.

The skill may be technically fine. The call may still be poorly managed.

The practice target is maintaining global awareness during tasks.

A useful orientation question is:

“What is changing while I am doing this?”

That question keeps the patient from disappearing behind the task.

## What instructors are often trying to show you

When instructors point out repeated errors, they are not only commenting on the scenario that just happened.

They are often trying to show a pattern they have seen forming across attempts.

That can feel uncomfortable. It may sound bigger than the student expected because, in a way, it is bigger. It is not only about one missed reassessment or one delayed decision. It is about the shape of the student’s thinking when pressure rises.

An instructor might say:

- “You keep waiting too long to name risk.”
- “You are doing good assessments, but you are not changing your plan when the patient changes.”
- “You recognize patterns quickly, but you close too early.”
- “You know the directive, but you are not applying the intent.”
- “Your skills are improving, but your situational awareness drops when you perform them.”

This kind of feedback gives students something valuable. It shows the pattern while it is still changeable.

The goal is not to feel good about the feedback. The goal is to make it specific enough to use.

## Turning an error into a practice target

A practice target should be narrow enough that you can carry it into the next scenario.

It should describe what you will notice or do differently.

Not:

“I need to improve clinical reasoning.”

Better:

“In the next scenario, I will name my working concern earlier, even if I am not certain.”

Not:

“I need to stop missing reassessment.”

Better:

“After each intervention, I will reassess the specific problem that made me intervene.”

Not:

“I need to understand directives better.”

Better:

“When reviewing a directive, I will identify what risk it is protecting against and what findings would make the intervention unsafe.”

This is where improvement becomes practical.

The error reveals the pattern. The pattern helps define the practice target. The practice target shapes the next attempt.

## Moving forward

Common errors matter because they show where learning is not yet stable under pressure.

They are not harmless, and they should not be brushed aside. They are also not proof that a student cannot do this work.

They are information that needs to be handled carefully.

Scenario days reveal the pattern. Feedback helps name it. The next step is to practise the right thing on purpose, without trying to rebuild everything at once.

## Glossary and Popup Terms

### error pattern

A repeated type of mistake that shows where a learning or reasoning process is not yet stable under pressure.

### cognitive load

The amount of mental effort being used at one time. In scenarios, high cognitive load can narrow attention and make familiar knowledge harder to access.

### premature closure

Settling on an explanation too early and failing to keep checking whether new information supports or challenges it.

### reassessment

Checking the patient again after time, treatment, movement, or new information to see whether the clinical picture has changed.

### working concern

A temporary best-fit explanation of what may be happening, used to guide assessment and action while uncertainty remains.

### feedback extraction

The process of turning feedback into one specific next action, rather than treating it as general criticism.

### deliberate practice

Focused practice aimed at improving one specific weakness, with feedback and repeated attempts.
