## App-Ready Section Draft

## Why premature closure is hard to catch

Premature closure does not usually feel like a mistake while it is happening.

It often feels like the call finally makes sense.

A patient resembles something familiar. One explanation starts to organize the scene. Your questions become more directed. Your treatment plan begins to form. After a few minutes of uncertainty, that can feel like relief.

That relief is understandable.

It is also where the risk begins.

Once a call has a shape, the brain wants to keep that shape. Details that fit become easier to notice. Details that do not fit become easier to explain away. The first explanation starts to feel stronger, not always because the evidence is stronger, but because everything is now being viewed through it.

This can happen to careful students.

It can happen to strong students.

It can happen because the first impression was reasonable.

Premature closure is not the same as making a wild guess. It is what happens when a possible explanation becomes too settled too early, and the rest of the call is no longer allowed to change it.

## Why early closure feels natural

Students are often told not to jump to conclusions.

That advice is useful, but it can make premature closure sound simpler than it is. It can make it sound like the fix is just to be more careful, more open-minded, or more disciplined.

In real scenarios, the pressure is more practical than that.

You need the call to make sense. You need to communicate with your partner. You need to choose priorities. You need to decide what matters now and what can wait. In an OSCE, you may also feel the evaluator watching every pause.

An early explanation helps reduce that strain.

It gives your assessment direction. It helps you choose questions. It lets you anticipate what equipment, treatment, transport decision, or reassessment might matter next.

That is why premature closure can be so tempting. It begins with something useful.

The problem starts when the explanation stops being flexible.

A working explanation says, “This is what seems most likely right now.”

Premature closure says, “This is the answer.”

Those can feel similar in the moment. They are not the same.

## What premature closure can look like

Premature closure does not always look like rushing.

Sometimes it does.

A student sees wheezing and immediately treats the whole call as asthma. They stop listening for signs that the patient is tiring, infected, allergic, or presenting with something more complicated.

Sometimes it looks like confidence.

A student hears chest pain and organizes every finding around cardiac ischemia. That concern may be appropriate, but the student stops paying attention to details that complicate the picture.

Sometimes it looks like being thorough.

A student decides a call is low acuity and keeps collecting history. They ask good questions, but they do not notice that the patient is becoming paler, slower to answer, or more unstable. The assessment continues, but it is no longer changing the plan.

Sometimes it looks like hesitation.

A student becomes attached to one explanation but does not fully trust it. Instead of widening the frame, they circle the same details again and again, hoping the call will eventually become clear enough to make action feel safe.

The outside behavior can look different.

The underlying issue is similar.

The student is no longer letting the patient update the explanation.

## A paramedic example

Consider a patient who appears anxious and short of breath.

You arrive to find a young adult sitting on the edge of a couch. They are breathing quickly and say they cannot calm down. Their hands are tingling. They have a history of panic attacks. A family member says, “This happens sometimes when they get overwhelmed.”

It would be reasonable for anxiety or panic to come to mind.

That impression may help you approach the patient calmly. It may help you reduce stimulation, slow the interaction down, and avoid escalating the patient’s distress. It may also prevent you from treating the presentation as more dramatic than it is.

But it can become unsafe if panic closes the call too early.

The patient is still tachypneic. They mention vague chest tightness. Their skin is slightly pale. They look worse when they stand. Their pulse is faster than expected. The oxygen saturation looks acceptable, but that does not explain everything. The story is familiar, but not clean.

If the student has already closed the call, those details may become background noise.

The tingling hands confirm panic.

The fast breathing confirms panic.

The family history confirms panic.

The student may continue reassurance without asking what else could produce this presentation. They may stop checking whether the patient is improving, tiring, compensating, or developing a different problem.

A safer approach is not to reject anxiety as a possibility.

A safer approach is to keep it provisional.

“This may be anxiety, but what would make that explanation unsafe to rely on?”

That question reopens the call.

It does not make the student dramatic. It does not mean every anxious patient is hiding something catastrophic. It simply keeps the early impression accountable to the rest of the assessment.

## Commitment is not the problem

Avoiding premature closure does not mean avoiding decisions.

This is important.

Some students hear warnings about premature closure and become reluctant to commit to anything. They keep every possibility open for too long. They avoid naming a concern. They wait for certainty because they do not want to be accused of jumping ahead.

That can create its own problem.

Patients still need care while the picture is incomplete. You may need to treat, transport, call for support, manage risk, or explain your concern before the final answer is obvious.

The issue is not commitment.

The issue is rigidity.

Commitment sounds like:

“Based on what I have right now, this is the safest plan.”

Rigidity sounds like:

“This is the answer, and I am going to make the rest of the call fit.”

Good care needs the first one.

It gets into trouble with the second.

## Signs that your thinking may be closing

Premature closure is easier to catch if you know some of its early signs.

Watch for moments when you notice yourself thinking:

- “This is obviously just...”
- “That finding probably does not matter.”
- “They always look like this when...”
- “I already know where this is going.”
- “I do not need to reassess that yet.”
- “The treatment did not help, but maybe it just needs more time.”

None of these thoughts automatically means you are wrong.

They are signals.

You may still be carrying the right explanation. The first impression may still hold. But if you feel yourself becoming dismissive, annoyed by conflicting information, or overly comfortable with one story, it is time to reopen the frame.

The patient does not need your first impression to be perfect.

They need your thinking to remain responsive.

## Take mismatch seriously

One of the most useful habits in clinical reasoning is noticing when something does not fit.

A mismatch is any piece of information that does not sit comfortably inside your current explanation.

The patient looks sicker than the story suggests.

The vital signs are drifting when you expected stability.

The treatment does not produce the response you expected.

A new piece of history complicates the pattern.

The scene does not match the complaint.

Students often notice these details, but do not always use them. Under pressure, mismatches can feel inconvenient. They interrupt the flow of a call that was starting to feel organized.

That inconvenience is useful.

When something does not fit, pause internally and ask what the mismatch could mean.

It may mean your explanation is wrong.

It may mean your explanation is incomplete.

It may mean there is a second problem.

It may mean the patient is changing.

Not every mismatch is an emergency. Not every mismatch should send you in a completely new direction. But it should be noticed before it is dismissed.

## Reassessment keeps the explanation honest

Reassessment is one of the strongest protections against premature closure.

Not because it is a required step on a form.

Because it gives the patient a chance to correct your thinking.

After an intervention, reassessment asks whether the patient responded in a way that fits your explanation.

After time passes, reassessment asks whether the patient’s trajectory still makes sense.

After new information appears, reassessment asks whether your working explanation still holds.

This is where early closure often becomes visible.

A respiratory patient becomes quieter after treatment. That may be improvement. It may also be fatigue.

A chest pain patient reports some improvement. That matters, but it does not erase the need to reassess vitals, risk, and transport priorities.

An anxious patient calms down. That is useful, but it does not automatically prove anxiety was the only problem.

Reassessment is not just repetition.

It is how you test whether the call is still behaving the way you thought it was.

## A simple reopening check

When you feel yourself becoming too certain, use a short reopening check.

Ask:

1. What explanation am I currently carrying?
2. What finding does not fit that explanation?
3. What is the highest-risk alternative I cannot miss?
4. Has the patient changed since I formed this impression?
5. What should I reassess before I keep going?

This is not meant to become a full diagnostic exercise.

It is a way to stop the call from becoming smaller than the patient.

Used well, it does not slow you down much. It may actually save time, because you stop spending attention defending an explanation that is starting to weaken.

## What instructors are often seeing

When instructors point out premature closure, they are usually not saying you were foolish for having an early impression.

You are supposed to form early impressions.

The concern is that the impression became too hard to move.

Instructors may notice that a student:

- ignores a changing vital sign because it does not fit the first impression
- keeps treating the same problem despite poor response
- stops reassessing after a familiar intervention
- explains away concerning findings too quickly
- fails to name a high-risk alternative
- becomes confident before the patient has earned that confidence

This is why feedback sometimes focuses less on what you did and more on what you did not reconsider.

The missed issue may not be knowledge.

It may be flexibility.

The student had enough information to reopen the call, but the first explanation had already become too settled.

## Practicing against premature closure

You can practice avoiding premature closure before you are in a scenario.

When reviewing a condition, ask what it can be mistaken for.

When comparing two presentations, ask what makes them look similar early.

When debriefing a scenario, ask where the call first started to feel obvious.

That last question matters.

Premature closure often begins at the moment the student feels the call settle.

Practice with comparisons like:

- asthma and pulmonary edema
- panic and pulmonary embolism
- hypoglycemia and stroke
- sepsis and dehydration
- ACS and reflux
- intoxication and head injury

For each pair, ask:

- What makes these look similar at first?
- What finding would separate them?
- What would be dangerous to assume?
- What response to treatment would make me reconsider?
- What would I need to reassess before trusting my first impression?

This kind of practice does not make you paranoid.

It makes you flexible.

You learn to hold an early explanation without gripping it too tightly.

## Moving forward

Premature closure is not a failure to think.

It is what happens when thinking stops updating after the call begins to make sense.

That distinction matters.

You want early impressions. You want patterns. You want your assessment to become organized. But the explanation has to remain open to correction.

## Glossary and Popup Terms

### Premature closure

Premature closure happens when you settle on an explanation too early and stop noticing information that should make you reconsider.

### Fixation

Fixation is when attention becomes stuck on one explanation, task, or cue. In scenarios, fixation can make other important information harder to notice.

### Disconfirming cue

A disconfirming cue is a finding that does not fit your current explanation. It may mean the explanation is wrong, incomplete, or needs to be reassessed.

### Cognitive narrowing

Cognitive narrowing is the tendency for attention to become tighter under pressure. It can help with focus, but it can also make students miss information outside the immediate task.

### Reassessment

Reassessment is the process of checking whether the patient, your plan, and your explanation still make sense after time passes or care is provided.

### Working explanation

A working explanation is your best current understanding of what is happening. It should guide care while remaining open to change.
